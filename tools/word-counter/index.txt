27:T10b5,The Word Counter shows seven numbers as you type: words, characters, characters without spaces, sentences, paragraphs, lines, and a reading-time estimate. Every one of them is recomputed on each keystroke from the text in the box, in your browser. There is no upload, no account, and no button to press. That makes it useful for the moment when a client says "no more than 800 words" and you need to know what the tool you are both looking at actually means by a word.

That last point is the reason this page exists. A word count is not a fact about a document; it is the output of a definition. This tool uses a deliberately simple one: trim the text, then split it on runs of whitespace. Concretely, the word count is text.trim().split(/\s+/).length, or zero when the text is empty or only whitespace. Runs of spaces, tabs, newlines, carriage returns, non-breaking spaces and full-width spaces all collapse into one separator. A zero-width space is not whitespace to JavaScript, so it does not split anything. Hyphens and apostrophes do not split either: well-known, don't and e-mail are each one word. Punctuation stays attached to the word next to it.

The consequence for Chinese, Japanese and Thai text is worth stating plainly, because it is the single biggest source of disagreement between word counters. A sentence such as 今天天气很好，我们去公园吧。 has no spaces, so this tool reports 1 word and 14 characters. The same sentence with spaces inserted between phrases reports 3 words. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions. If you are quoting a limit for CJK copy, agree on whether the unit is whitespace-delimited tokens or characters before you start, and use the character count when the limit comes from a platform that counts characters. Our character-counting guide in the blog goes into why code units, code points and grapheme clusters give three different answers for the same emoji.

Two character figures are shown side by side on purpose. Characters is text.length, which counts UTF-16 code units rather than visible symbols: a plain 😀 counts as 2, and the family emoji 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 counts as 11 because it is four emoji joined by three zero-width joiners. Characters (no spaces) applies text.replace(/\s/g, "") — it removes whitespace and nothing else, so commas, full stops, emoji and zero-width joiners all remain in the number. A string like "Hi 😀" is 5 characters, 4 without spaces, and 2 words. If a form field enforces a byte limit rather than a character limit, note that UTF-8 uses 3 bytes for 中 and 4 for 😀, so a character count will not predict it.

Sentences, paragraphs and lines each follow their own rule, and they do not have to agree. Sentences come from splitting on one or more of . ! or ?, so Dr. Smith arrived at 3.14 pm. Really! reports 7 words but 4 sentences: the period in Dr., the decimal point in 3.14, the period after pm, and the exclamation mark all create boundaries. Because the pattern uses a + quantifier, an ellipsis counts as one break, and a trailing piece of text with no final punctuation still counts as a sentence. Full-width punctuation such as 。 is not in the pattern, so a Chinese paragraph is one sentence. Paragraphs require a blank line: text.split(/\n\s*\n/) means a single newline stays inside the same paragraph, while four consecutive newlines still produce just two paragraphs. Lines is a plain split on newline, which is why an empty textarea reports 1 line, not 0.

Reading time is Math.ceil(words / 200): a fixed 200 words per minute, rounded up. One word and 200 words both show 1 min; 201 words shows 2 min. It is a rough planning number for a single reader, not a prediction of how long a real audience will spend, and it inherits every quirk of the word definition above — a Chinese article will report a far shorter reading time than the same content translated into English. To be explicit about what this tool does not do: there is no speaking-time estimate, no unique-word count, no average word length, no keyword density and no per-language tokenizer. Those figures appear in some other counters; they are not computed here.
