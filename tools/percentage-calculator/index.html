About the Percentage Calculator

The Percentage Calculator answers the three questions people constantly confuse: what is X percent of Y, X is what percent of Y, and what is the percentage change from one number to another. Getting the right one matters, because the wrong choice produces an answer that looks plausible and is wrong by a factor of the base. It also computes the reverse case, where you know the final value and the percentage and need the original number, which comes up whenever a price includes tax or a figure has already been marked down.

Where people use the Percentage Calculator

  • Checking a discount against the price on the shelf.
  • Working out the growth rate between two months of figures.
  • Converting a test score into a percentage.
  • Finding the pre-tax amount when only the gross figure is known.

How to use the Percentage Calculator

  1. 1

    Identify which question you are asking

    A part of a whole, a ratio expressed as a percentage, or a change between two values. Each has its own field set.

  2. 2

    Enter the two numbers

    For a change, the first value is the starting point and the second is the ending point. Order matters here and reversing it flips the sign of the answer.

  3. 3

    Read the result and the working

    The tool shows the arithmetic as well as the answer, so you can check it against your own reasoning.

  4. 4

    Check the direction of a change

    An increase from 80 to 100 is 25 percent, while a decrease from 100 to 80 is 20 percent. The same 20 unit gap is a different percentage depending on which end you start from.

Tips and best practices

  • When comparing rates, say percentage points out loud to yourself before writing the sentence.
  • For a chain of changes, multiply the factors rather than adding the percentages.
  • Round only at the end; rounding each step compounds the error.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between percent and percentage points?

A change from 4 percent to 6 percent is an increase of 2 percentage points, but a 50 percent increase in the rate itself. News reporting mixes these up constantly. Use percentage points when comparing two rates, and percent when describing relative growth.

Why is a 20 percent discount followed by 20 percent tax not back to the original price?

Because each percentage applies to a different base. Multiplying by 0.8 then 1.2 gives 0.96, so you end up 4 percent below where you started. Percentages only add and subtract cleanly when the base stays the same.

How do I find the original price before a discount?

Divide rather than subtract. If something costs 80 after 20 percent off, the original is 80 divided by 0.8, which is 100. Subtracting 20 percent from 80 gives 64, which is the common mistake.

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value, then multiply by 100. So a move from 250 to 300 is 50 divided by 250, which is 0.2, or 20 percent.

Why do percentages over 100 appear?

They are legitimate whenever the new value exceeds the original. Sales rising from 10 to 25 is a 150 percent increase. A percentage above 100 only looks wrong when someone means a proportion of a whole, which cannot exceed 100.
