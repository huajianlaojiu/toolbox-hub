6 Free Math Tools for Numbers That Will Not Behave

Scientific notation, decimals to fractions, prime checking, factorials and GCF/LCM — free browser tools for the number work that is easy to get wrong.

The maths you actually need at a computer is not the maths you were examined on. It is: is this number in the format the system expects, is this fraction simplified, how fast does this grow, and why does the calculator and the spreadsheet disagree.

These six tools cover that territory. They run locally, which for arithmetic means one specific benefit worth naming: the number you typed is not sent anywhere, so you can check a calculation involving a salary, a balance or a customer figure without a second thought.

Getting numbers into a form a machine accepts

Computers store decimal fractions as binary fractions. 0.1 is not exactly representable in binary — the closest double-precision value is 0.1000000000000000055511151231257827. Add 0.1 + 0.2 and you get 0.30000000000000004, which prints as 0.3 by default and quietly breaks an equality check.

  • Scientific Notation Converter — move between standard and scientific form, which is how most logs, spreadsheets and APIs actually store large and small numbers.
  • Decimal to Fraction — convert a decimal into an exact fraction. This is the fix for the problem above: 0.1 + 0.2 in exact fraction arithmetic is 3/10, not an approximation.

If you have ever seen a total that is wrong by 0.0000000000000004, this is why. In financial code the standard answer is to work in integer cents, or in a decimal library, and never in floating point. If you have ever seen a spreadsheet disagree with a script by a fraction of a penny, it is the same bug wearing a different hat.

Scientific notation is worth being fluent in, because it is the difference between reading a log line and squinting at it. 6.022e23, 1.6e-19 and 3e8 are Avogadro's number, the elementary charge and the speed of light. If those look like noise rather than numbers, converting a few through the tool fixes it permanently.

Primes, and why anyone cares

  • Prime Number Checker — test whether a number is prime, and see its factorisation when it is not.
  • GCF LCM Calculator — greatest common factor and least common multiple.

Prime numbers are not a school exercise. The security of every HTTPS connection you make depends on the fact that multiplying two large primes is easy and factoring the result back is hard. That asymmetry is the entire basis of RSA.

For everyday purposes, prime checking is mostly about fractions. Simplifying a fraction means dividing top and bottom by their greatest common factor, and you cannot find that without factoring. Which is why these two tools belong together: check the factors, then get the GCF and LCM in one step.

The LCM half is the one people forget, and it is the one that solves scheduling problems: two events that repeat every 18 days and every 12 days coincide every 36 days. Same arithmetic, different application.

Factorials, and the speed at which they explode

  • Factorial Calculator — n! for values where the answer is still meaningful.

Factorials grow faster than almost anything you meet at a desk. 10! is about 3.6 million. 20! is about 2.4 quintillion — larger than the number of grains of sand on Earth by a wide margin. 52!, the number of ways to shuffle a deck of cards, is roughly 8 followed by 67 digits.

The practical consequence is that a program that enumerates all permutations is fine at 5 items, tolerable at 8, and permanently stuck at 12. If a task feels like it will never finish and you are looping over permutations, this is almost certainly why. It is also the argument against most brute-force approaches, stated as arithmetic rather than opinion.

Roman numerals, and the odd thing about them

  • Roman Numeral Converter — convert to and from Roman numerals.

Roman numerals are worth understanding rather than just converting, because the system reveals something about positional notation. There is no zero, and no place value: XI means eleven whether it represents eleven sticks or eleven thousand. That is why arithmetic in Roman numerals is genuinely difficult, and why the system survived for centuries mostly for counting and dating rather than calculation.

The subtractive rule is the part people get wrong. IV is four, not VI; IX is nine. Clocks are a famous exception — Big Ben uses IIII rather than IV, for reasons historians still argue about. Converting rather than deriving is the right call in every case.

A worked example: checking a fraction by hand

A recipe needs 0.375 cups and a scale that reads in grams, a report quotes a ratio as 0.625, and both look like numbers somebody typed in a hurry.

  • 1. Find the exact fraction. Run 0.375 through the Decimal to Fraction tool: 3/8. Run 0.625: 5/8. Neither needed a calculator habit you do not have.
  • 2. Simplify the ratio. To express 3/8 against 5/8, you need the GCF of 3 and 5. The GCF LCM Calculator gives 1, so the ratio is 3:5 and already simplest.
  • 3. Check the factorials claim. If someone tells you a queue of 9 orders has 362,880 arrangements, the Factorial Calculator confirms 9! is exactly that. Now you know whether the build-time estimate was plausible.
  • 4. Normalise anything large. A server reporting 1.5e9 nanoseconds is 1.5 seconds, per the Scientific Notation Converter — which is the difference between a latency problem and a rounding artefact.

What these tools deliberately do not do

They do not show working, and they do not teach. If you need to understand why a formula is true, a calculator is the wrong instrument — it gives the answer without the reason, which is how people end up able to compute and unable to reason.

They also do not do arbitrary precision. Factorials and large factorisations will lose accuracy past the point where the answer is still useful, and a browser tab is not a computer algebra system. For anything where correctness genuinely matters — engineering, finance, cryptography — use a proper library with exact arithmetic, not a web page.

Checking your own arithmetic

The most valuable mathematical habit is not speed, it is estimation. Before believing any result, ask whether it is the right order of magnitude: bigger or smaller than the input, and by roughly how much. Most arithmetic mistakes survive every other check except this one.

Rounding first makes estimation free. Round 0.375 to 0.4, round 5/8 to 0.6, and the ratio is obviously about 2:3 — close enough to 3:5 to confirm the fraction you computed exactly. If the exact answer and the estimate disagree, you have found an input error rather than a rounding problem, and you found it in seconds.

This is also why a calculator is not a substitute for thinking about what you asked it. A tool will compute the wrong quantity perfectly, and the only defence is knowing what the answer should feel like.

Frequently asked questions

Is my input sent anywhere? No. All of these run locally in your browser.

Why does 0.1 plus 0.2 not equal 0.3? Because neither 0.1 nor 0.2 is exactly representable in binary floating point. The sum is 0.30000000000000004, which is the closest double to 0.3.

How large a factorial can these handle? Up to 170 before exceeding the range of a double. Past that the answer is displayed in scientific notation and then stops being meaningful.

Can I use the output in code? Yes. For the decimal-to-fraction results in particular, use integer arithmetic on the numerator and denominator rather than converting back to a decimal.

The one-line version

Use exact fractions when money is involved, remember that factorials explode faster than you expect, and prime factorisation is both a security foundation and a fraction-simplifying trick.
