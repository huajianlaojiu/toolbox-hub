14 Free Image and Design Tools That Run in Your Browser

Compress, resize and prepare images, and generate CSS effects, palettes, contrast checks and favicons without installing Photoshop or a design suite.

Image work has a specific problem: the job is usually small, and the tools on offer are enormous. You need to shave 400 KB off a hero image, or get a hex value your designer mentioned, or generate a favicon nobody has made yet. That does not require a subscription.

The fourteen tools below cover preparing images, working with colour, generating CSS effects, and the small production assets that fall through the cracks. Every one runs locally, which matters more for images than for text: the photo you just compressed is often a photo you have not published yet.

Preparing images for the web

Most image problems are really weight problems. A page that feels slow is usually carrying images that are larger than their display size, in the wrong format, at a quality setting nobody ever questioned.

  • Image Compressor — reduce file size while keeping the image visually intact. Run it before you upload anything to a form with a size limit.
  • Image Resizer — set the actual pixel dimensions you need. This is the bigger win: a 4000px image displayed at 800px is carrying five times the data it needs.
  • Image to Base64 — convert a small image into a data URI for inlining in CSS, HTML or an email template.

The order matters. Resize first, compress second. Compressing a 4000px image produces a much heavier file than resizing to 800px and then compressing at the same quality, because compressors spend their effort on pixels that were going to be thrown away anyway.

How much should you compress? The honest answer is: until it looks wrong, then back off one step. For a photographic hero image, 70 to 80 percent quality is usually indistinguishable from the original at normal viewing size. For a logo, a screenshot or anything with flat colour and hard edges, use PNG and compress harder — these gain more and lose less.

A practical rule of thumb: anything under 200 KB is fine for a hero image, under 80 KB for a thumbnail, and if you cannot get under 300 KB you probably have a dimension problem rather than a quality problem.

Base64: when inlining is the right call

Converting an image to a Base64 data URI removes an HTTP request, which sounds like a pure win. It usually is not. The encoded string is roughly a third larger than the binary file, it cannot be cached separately, and it bloats your CSS or HTML.

Inline only when the image is small — under a few kilobytes — used once, and critical. A tiny icon in a single email template is the classic case. For anything repeated across pages or larger than a few kilobytes, serve it as a file and let the browser cache it.

Watermarks: what is honestly removable

  • Image Watermark Remover — crop or blur a mark area, which handles a corner logo or a repeated diagonal overlay.

Be clear about what this does. It removes or obscures the visible mark. It cannot reconstruct the underlying pixels — information that was painted over is gone, and no filter invents it back. Results range from invisible, when the mark sits over a plain background, to an obvious smear when it sits over detail.

One more thing worth saying plainly: removing somebody else's watermark and publishing the result is a copyright problem in most jurisdictions, and a licensing violation regardless of whether you would get caught. Removing marks from your own images, or from stock you have licensed, is the case this tool is for.

Colour: converting, checking, choosing

Colour is where small mistakes become very visible. A contrast ratio that fails accessibility, a hex value that is actually an HSL triple, a palette that looks coherent on your monitor and muddy on a projector.

  • Color Converter — move between HEX, RGB and HSL. HSL is worth learning to read, because hue 210, saturation 40 percent, lightness 50 percent is far easier to adjust by hand than a hex value.
  • Contrast Checker — test text against its background against WCAG thresholds. This is the single most useful tool in this article, because it catches a real compliance problem rather than a taste problem.
  • Color Palette Generator — generate a coherent set when you are starting from a brand colour and need the rest of the scale.

On contrast: WCAG AA asks for 4.5 to 1 for normal body text and 3 to 1 for large text. If you are choosing between two brand colours for a button, the accessible one is usually not the one the designer likes. Check first, then have the argument with data.

One caveat on colour tools: they all assume your monitor is calibrated and that the person viewing your page has working eyes. Neither assumption is safe. That is a reason to prefer contrast-checked combinations over subjective ones.

CSS effects without opening a design tool

Writing a gradient or a layered shadow by hand is a genuinely unpleasant task. The syntax is fiddly, you cannot see what you are producing, and the difference between a good shadow and a bad one is a few pixels of blur.

  • CSS Gradient Generator — build linear and radial gradients and copy the CSS directly.
  • Box Shadow Generator — layer multiple shadows, which is what separates a subtle elevation from a heavy drop.
  • Border Radius Generator — get the asymmetric radii that make a shape look intentional.
  • Text Shadow Generator — useful sparingly. Most text shadows are too strong; this lets you tune the offset and blur until it disappears.

The trick to all four is restraint. Two shadows — a tight dark one and a wide soft one — read as physical elevation. One shadow reads as a website from fifteen years ago. Generate, copy, then delete until you cannot tell it is there.

The production assets everyone forgets

  • Placeholder Image Generator — generate a sized placeholder with a colour or text, for wireframes and templates where a real image is not ready.
  • Favicon Generator — produce the small icon set a site actually needs. This is on the list because favicons are the asset most likely to be missing entirely, and a missing one shows a generic browser page icon that makes a finished site look unfinished.
  • Emoji Picker — browse, search and copy emoji without hunting through an operating system picker. Genuinely useful when you are writing copy that needs one specific symbol.

A worked example: shipping a hero section

Here is the whole cluster doing one real job. A page needs a hero with a background image, a heading and a button, and none of it has been built yet.

  • 1. Placeholder. Drop a sized placeholder in from the Placeholder Image Generator so the layout has the right height from the first render.
  • 2. Resize and compress. When the real photo arrives, run it through the Image Resizer at 1600px wide, then the Image Compressor at 75 percent. That is a file you can ship.
  • 3. Contrast-check the text. A heading over a photo is the classic contrast failure. Use the Contrast Checker to pick either a dark overlay strength or a light text colour that passes AA.
  • 4. Build the button. The Box Shadow Generator gives the elevation, and the Color Converter gets the brand colour into the format the CSS needs.
  • 5. Ship. Add a favicon from the Favicon Generator so the tab does not look abandoned, and the section is done.

Five tools, no design suite, and every output is a value you can paste straight into code.

Frequently asked questions

Are my images uploaded anywhere? No. Compression, resizing and Base64 conversion all happen in your browser, which is the reason there is no file-size limit and no upload queue.

Will compressing an image lose quality? Always, by definition. The question is whether it is visible. At 70 to 80 percent quality and correct dimensions, it usually is not.

Why does my resized image still look large? Nine times out of ten you compressed before resizing, or you are saving as PNG when a photo is better as JPEG.

Can I use these for client work? Yes, with no attribution and no commercial restriction.

Do they work on a phone? Yes, though resizing and compressing large photos on a phone depends on your device's memory rather than a server.

The one-line version

Resize before you compress, check contrast before you argue about colour, keep shadows subtle, and never forget the favicon. That is most of what a design suite was doing for you anyway.
